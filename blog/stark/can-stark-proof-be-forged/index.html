Can a STARK Proof Be Forged or Replayed Across Programs?

Can a STARK proof be forged or replayed? What soundness actually guarantees, where replay risks live, and how vkey pinning plus freshness stop cross-program reuse.

What soundness actually promises

STARK soundness is a bound: at 96-bit security, a forger's success probability per attempt is about 2^-96, and attempts are expensive (each requires grinding work). The bound rests on named assumptions -- hash collision resistance, proximity amplification, Fiat-Shamir heuristic soundness -- all public, all analyzed, none secret. Contrast with ceremony-based systems where the assumption list includes human process integrity; the trusted-setup Q&A sizes that difference precisely. Within its assumptions, a STARK proof is as forgeable as SHA-2 is breakable: the honest answer to "can it be forged" is "not by anyone whose budget fits in this universe".

The attacks that are real: substitution and replay

  • Program substitution: proof of program B presented as program A. Defeated by vkey pinning -- the verifier asserts the program-image hash before evaluation. Without it, structural validity is not identity.
  • Input replay: an old valid proof re-presented for a new claim. Defeated by binding freshness into public inputs (timestamps, nonces, claim ids) so old proofs verify only for old claims.

How parameters change the answer

Soundness is a budget, and budgets can be misconfigured: dropping grinding from 16 to 8 bits halves the work per forgery attempt; shrinking query counts trades bytes for error probability; a 64-bit field under a 128-bit claim overstates security by design. Honest systems publish parameters in every manifest and assert them at verification, so weakening is visible to every verifier rather than profitable to an attacker. When auditing any proof system, request the parameter derivation: a vendor who cannot produce it is selling either ignorance or negligence, and both price into your risk register at the same rate.

Quantum footnote, sized again

Grover-style attacks halve hash-security bits (96 becomes ~48-equivalent against quantum adversaries in worst-case analyses), which is why long-lived artifacts target 128-bit parameters today -- cheap insurance bought once at proof time. No known quantum attack breaks FRI's combinatorics beyond that halving story, and Fiat-Shamir's quantum-status research remains favorable for standard parameterizations, per the citations maintained on the research page. The practical guidance never changes: pin identity, publish parameters, archive proofs with manifests, and let future verifiers re-check under their era's assumptions -- composition and record-keeping as the true post-quantum strategy.
