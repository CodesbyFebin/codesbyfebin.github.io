The FRI Protocol Explained Simply: Commitments, Folds, Queries

The job FRI does in one paragraph

Constraints reduce correctness to "this committed table is a low-degree polynomial evaluated on a domain". Commitments (Merkle roots) let the verifier request authenticated values, but Merkle alone cannot answer the global question "is the underlying function low-degree?" -- a table of random noise commits just fine. FRI answers exactly that question probabilistically: it does not read the polynomial, it interrogates its structure through folds and samples. The soundness statement is proximity: if the committed function is far from any polynomial of the claimed degree, FRI rejects except with negligible probability; if it is close, the decoded polynomial is unique, and the constraints evaluated on it carry the computational claim. Everything else in a STARK is bookkeeping around this interrogation.

Folding: halving with a challenge

Write the polynomial in even/odd parts, f(x) = e(x²) + x·o(x²). Given a verifier challenge α, define the folded function f₁(y) = e(y) + α·o(y): degree halves, and knowing f₁ plus the commitment to f ties the two together at paired points ±x. Repeat log-times until a constant remains. Each layer is committed before the next challenge is derived (Fiat-Shamir from the transcript), so the prover cannot adapt layers to future folds. The verifier's work per query is a pairwise consistency check down the layer stack: open f at ±x, open f₁ at x², check the folding relation with α -- log-depth arithmetic over Merkle paths, which is why verification stays millisecond-fast even for million-row traces, as the measurements in the STARK overview show.

Queries, grinding, and where the bytes go

DEEP queries: sampling where it matters

Plain FRI samples the committed trace at random domain points, but constraint checks happen at composition-polynomial points chosen after commitments -- the DEEP (Domain Extension for Eliminating Pretenders) technique extends the sampling domain so that openings at those out-of-domain points bind the trace exactly where the constraints are evaluated. Intuitively: without DEEP, a cheating prover could keep constraints honest at sampled spots while violating them between them; with DEEP, the out-of-domain openings interpolate global behavior, closing the gap. Implementers meet DEEP as an extra alignment step between constraint evaluation and query opening; reviewers should meet it as the reason "sampled constraints" is a sound notion at all -- the detail most "FRI explained" sketches skip and every real prover implements, Winterfell included, as the tutorial's constraint section assumes.

A mental picture that survives contact

Picture a torn receipt: each fold presses the paper in half, ink bleeding sums of overlapping lines; the verifier cannot read the whole receipt, but by holding random creases up to the light -- Merkle paths as the light -- they confirm every crease aligns with the fold pattern the challenges dictate. A forged receipt would need misalignment hidden in a constant fraction of creases, and the light falls where the forger cannot predict. The picture carries the three properties that matter: commitments make ink inspectable, folds make inspection logarithmic, and random challenges make cheating a constant-fraction event rather than a needle event. When parameters confuse you later, return to the creases: query count is how many creases you hold up, grinding is shaking the paper before choosing, and proof size is simply the stack of illuminated folds.

When you later read parameter tables -- rate, folding arity, query counts -- map each back to a crease property: rate sets how much redundancy the light has to work with, arity sets how fast the paper halves, queries set how many creases you inspect. The vocabulary stops being arcane the moment it stops being abstract, which is the entire pedagogical bet of this cluster's ordering: pictures first, parameters second, proofs of the pictures in the literature citations maintained on the research page.
