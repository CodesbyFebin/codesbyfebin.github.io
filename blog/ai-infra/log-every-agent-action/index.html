Should I Log Every Agent Action? What Goes in the Journal?

Should you log every agent action? What belongs in an append-only journal, retention math, privacy scrubbing and how receipts make logs proof-grade.

Journal schema: seven fields, zero opinions

Hash-and-size args keep the journal readable at scale and privacy-defensible by default; full args stay in a tier-2 store with access logging, opened only under incident review policy. The reason field is the planner's own text captured at decision time -- post-hoc summaries lie, contemporaneous quotes do not, and the gap between reason and outcome is where misbehavior becomes visible: a write justified by "user asked" with no user turn in the session transcript is an injection signature, detectable by joining two logs nobody thought to join.

The three disciplines the journal enables

  • Forensics: session replay as a query (all calls, verdicts, reasons in order); incident timelines assembled in minutes instead of archaeology weeks.
  • Calibration: denial histograms per tool and class, approval-stamp rates, reason-versus-classification disagreement sampling -- the measurement loop from the onboarding Q&A runs entirely off journal aggregates.
  • Trust accounting: monthly per-session summaries (calls by class, writes by scope tier, denials) published to the humans who own the agent; trust that is reported survives, trust that is assumed expires at the first incident.

Journal operations: append-only, shipped, sampled

Append-only with hash-chaining (each entry references the previous hash) makes silent edits detectable -- the integrity trick from the federation manifests, applied to logs; ship to the backup target nightly because local journals die with local disks per the offsite guide; retain hot ninety days, cold per policy with the access log of every cold open. Sample weekly: twenty random write-class entries read by a human comparing reason to outcome -- the sample is cheap, and the knowledge that a sample exists changes how everyone writes reasons, which is the cheapest behavioral control in the entire stack.

Alerts off the journal, not off the agent

Four standing alerts: denial-code two-sigma spikes (schema confusion or capability gap), write-scope tier escalation (a session touching a higher tier than its capability row -- always an incident or a taxonomy bug), reason-absence on write-class calls (planner invoking without traceable justification), and journal-gap detection (hash-chain discontinuity or shipping failure). Each alert names an owner in the registry beside the tool; unowned alerts are wallpaper, and wallpaper is how journals become compliance artifacts instead of instruments -- the difference between a cockpit and a museum, which is the difference this entire cluster keeps drawing between systems that watch and systems that merely record.
