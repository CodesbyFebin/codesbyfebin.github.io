What leaves your machine today

Every cloud-assisted completion, summary, or chat call transmits prompt content -- which for developers means source code, for lawyers means drafts, for doctors means notes -- into a provider's processing pipeline, retention policy, and legal jurisdiction, plus whatever their sub-processors see. Even with "zero retention" contracts, the transmission itself creates exposure you cannot audit: TLS terminates at their load balancer, prompts sit in their queues, and abuse-review pipelines exist precisely to look at content. None of this is scandal; it is architecture. The point is that it is their architecture, and privacy begins where your control begins -- the same sovereignty argument the exit-the-cloud guide makes for storage, applied to cognition.

A threat model you can actually run

  • Removed: transit interception, vendor retention and training-use policy changes, subpoena exposure at the provider, pricing or ToS shocks that delete your assistant overnight.
  • Reduced: prompt-injection supply chains (local tooling can be gated offline, per the agent deletion Q&A), metadata leakage about usage patterns.
  • Unchanged: endpoint compromise, your own editor's cloud sync, screenshots, and the classic channel -- pasting secrets into prompts anyway. Local inference fixes the wire, not the workstation.

The practical local stack

Hardware first: the VRAM math Q&A sizes GPUs (7B Q4 in ~5 GB) and the 8 GB RAM Q&A covers CPU-only boxes; most households already own one capable machine. Serving: llama.cpp for personal scale, vLLM when the family becomes a user base, per the inference guide. Gateway: even a single-user setup benefits from the localhost OpenAI-compatible endpoint, because every client (editors, chat UIs, agent frameworks) speaks it, and switching models becomes a config line rather than a migration. Policy: wrap agent-facing endpoints in capability gates -- the XFree gate defaults network to deny, so "local model" cannot quietly become "local model phoning home for embeddings".

The honest capability ledger

Starting this week

One machine, one 7B Q4 model, one localhost endpoint, one client switched over. Measure for a month which requests you actually make (the gateway log answers), then choose the second model from evidence -- a coder variant or a larger generalist -- rather than from benchmark theater. The habit of asking "did this need to leave the building?" transfers to every other tool in your stack, which is the real dividend: local inference is less a product than a practice, and practices, unlike products, compound. For the quantization choices that set your quality floor, the quantization explainer in this cluster carries the measured deltas.

Extend the same question to adjacent tooling once the first month of logs exists: dictation, translation, OCR, and completion each have local variants of varying quality, and the gateway pattern generalizes -- one localhost endpoint per capability, one journal for all, one consent gate for exceptions. Households that finish the sweep report the same surprise: the hard part was not deployment but inventory, discovering how many quiet clouds lived inside daily tools. That inventory, once written, becomes the roadmap; each row is a weekend project with a measured privacy dividend, and the habit of asking survives precisely because the answers are visible in your own logs instead of asserted in someone's privacy policy -- and a roadmap you built from your own traffic is the only one nobody can deprecate out from under you.

Is my prompt data safer if the model is also downloaded once?

Yes, marginally: a cached, hash-verified model artifact removes repeat-download metadata and supply-chain drift between sessions. Verify weights against published hashes at download and pin them in your gateway config -- a model swap is a behavior change, and pinned hashes make it visible, exactly as vkey pinning does for proofs in the STARK cluster.

What about privacy for the model's training data?

A separate question with a separate ledger: weight licensing and provenance are ethical and legal choices independent of inference privacy. Prefer model cards with disclosed datasets, respect license clauses (some forbid commercial use), and record your selection rationale -- sovereignty over your prompts does not excuse indifference to how the weights were born.
