Send and Sync: the compiler's ownership geography

Rc<T> is neither Send nor Sync: the type system forbids moving or sharing it across threads because a non-atomic increment from two cores is undefined behavior waiting. Arc<T> is Send+Sync when T is, which is precisely the permission slip tokio tasks and std::thread::spawn demand. Practical reading: reaching for Arc is often the compiler teaching you that ownership crosses a thread boundary you created; sometimes the better lesson is removing the crossing (move the work to one owner task and pass messages, per the shared-state Q&A) rather than paying atomics forever to sustain a boundary the design does not need.

Weak references and the cycle trap (identical in both)

Rc and Arc share their one real hazard: a cycle of strong references never reaches zero, leaking the graph silently. The discipline is structural -- children hold Weak links to parents, caches hold Weak so eviction works, and any long-lived registry of Arc-holders gets a review question: "who breaks the cycle?" Detection is unglamorous and effective: a strong-count assertion in debug builds (debug_assert_eq!(Rc::strong_count(&x), expected)) plus a memory-growth alert in production. Neither pointer type protects you here; ownership hygiene does, which is why the systems guide treats object-graph truthfulness as the root skill from which both types' correct use follows.

The decision rule, in one ladder

  1. Ownership never shared? Use plain values or references -- no counter at all.
  2. Shared within one thread? Rc.
  3. Shared across threads, read-dominated? Arc (with RwLock or lock-free internals as needed).
  4. Shared counter or flag under contention? Atomics or a sharded design, not Arc-swapping.
  5. Cache or registry? Weak handles so liveness is decided by real owners.

Notice what the ladder never asks: "which is faster in the abstract?" The type follows the ownership geography; performance work follows the profiler. Teams that internalize this stop debating Rc-versus-Arc in review and start drawing the ownership map -- after which the pointer type is typography, and the map is the engineering.

Does Arc slow down reads of the pointed-to data?

No: the atomic protects only the count, not the payload. Reading through an Arc is as fast as through an Rc or a reference; interior mutability costs come from whatever synchronization you wrap (Mutex, RwLock, atomics), not from the A. This distinction matters in review, where "Arc is slow" usually misdiagnoses a lock held too long.

When is Arc<T> with interior atomics better than Mutex<T>?

When updates are per-field and independent (counters, flags, timestamps): lock-free atomics avoid convoy effects and keep readers wait-free. When updates are multi-field transactions needing consistency, Mutex wins -- atomics composing into torn states is the classic over-optimization bug. The tiebreaker is whether a reader can tolerate seeing fields from different instants; if not, you need the lock's transaction, not the atomic's speed.

Can I start with Rc and switch to Arc later?

Usually yes, mechanically (type alias or find-replace) -- which is the argument for starting with Rc in single-threaded prototypes: fewer atomics in flamegraphs while shapes settle, and the compiler enumerates every site that must change when threads arrive. The exception is types embedded in public APIs, where the pointer type is semver surface; there, decide deliberately and document, because churn in a published signature costs users more than nanoseconds cost you.
