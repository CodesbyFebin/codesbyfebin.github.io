What Happens to My Home Server During a Power Loss?

What happens to a home server during power loss: filesystem journaling, ZFS intent logs, UPS sizing, shutdown automation and post-outage verification steps.

What actually happens in the seconds and minutes

Modern journaling filesystems (ext4, XFS, btrfs, ZFS) survive abrupt loss better than folklore claims: journals and intent logs replay at mount, and ZFS's copy-on-write plus intent log make torn writes structurally impossible -- what dies mid-flight is in-flight application state: a half-written upload, an interrupted database transaction (usually rolled back cleanly at recovery, sometimes expensively), a backup repository left locked, and any process that cached state in memory without fsync. The infamous killers are rarer and specific: power-loss during firmware or bootloader writes, and cheap PSUs taking hardware with them on the way out. Both are procurement and update-window problems, not filesystem problems, which is why the cluster's production checklist schedules firmware updates on UPS-verified windows only.

When power returns: the boot-time verification

The dangerous minutes are after restoration: surges and brown-outs stress PSUs, RAID/ZFS pools import with stale members if a disk controller reset oddly, and services race to start against dependencies that are not ready. A boot-time verification unit answers all of it in order: pool import health (zpool status clean or alert), filesystem checks where the FS requests them, service dependency ordering via systemd wants/after (database before app, app before proxy), and a heartbeat emission so your monitoring knows the box returned -- silence after a known outage pages you, per the free-monitoring guide's absence-first philosophy. Fifteen lines of boot script convert "the lights are back" into "the estate is verified back", which is the sentence you want before coffee.

Long outages: planning the dark hours

Beyond runtime, decisions should be made calmly in advance: which services matter on battery (monitoring and mesh, arguably) versus which stop immediately (backups mid-run, media, batch jobs); whether the box wakes autonomously or waits for human decision (AC-restore policy); and what the household does about connectivity when the ISP's own battery budget ends (LTE fallback router on its own UPS, or acceptance of darkness). Write these as a one-page power policy beside the runbook; the kerala-mesh pilot's monsoon-season playbooks are exactly this document at community scale, and their field truth generalizes downward perfectly: households with a written power policy experience outages as procedures, and households without one experience them as discoveries.

Do SSDs or HDDs survive power loss better?

Modern SSDs with power-loss protection capacitors (enterprise and quality consumer NVMe) flush caches reliably; cheap DRAM-less SSDs historically had firmware-level loss bugs, now rare but not mythical. HDDs park heads on power-loss detection using rotational energy -- mechanically elegant and generally safe. The honest ranking puts filesystem choice (ZFS/btrfs with intent logs) above media choice for data integrity, and PSU quality above both for hardware survival.

Is a generator worth it for a homelab?

Only where multi-day outages are climatologically normal and your services are genuinely load-bearing (medical, remote-work primary income). For most homes, clean-stop-plus-wake beats generator complexity and fuel logistics; the middle path is a second, larger UPS rotated for battery health, buying an hour of ride-out for the services you flagged battery-critical in the power policy.

What about brown-outs and surges specifically?

Brown-outs (sustained low voltage) stress PSUs and trip UPSes into battery repeatedly -- a line-interactive UPS with AVR corrects most without battery cycling, extending battery life materially. Surges are handled upstream: a whole-home surge device at the panel plus point-of-use protection for the rack, replaced after any major strike event (their sacrifice is consumable). Log both events via NUT/AVR telemetry so battery replacements follow evidence rather than calendars.
