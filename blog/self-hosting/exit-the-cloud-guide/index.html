Read the labor line carefully: at three hours a week valued at $15/h, self-hosting totals about $3,310 versus $4,400 managed -- a real but unglamorous 25%. At one automated hour a week it becomes 60%. The gap between those two numbers is exactly what backups-as-code, IaC, and monitoring buy you, which is why this guide treats automation as cost control rather than virtue. Workloads with bursty compute invert the math entirely; those stay cloud, and saying so is part of an honest exit plan.

Migration order: reverse-risk, not reverse-importance

  1. Stateless consumers first: static sites, dashboards, API clients. Zero data at risk, maximum learning about your own network path.
  2. Stateful-but-rebuildable: caches, queues, CI runners. Losing a night's work is a story, not an incident.
  3. Identity and secrets: password manager, CA, SSH bastion -- move these with ceremony, dual-running until every device has re-registered.
  4. Primary state last: mail, photos, documents, databases. By now your restore drills, monitoring, and on-call habits (yours, singular) are proven on lesser workloads.

The ordering rule is simple: never migrate a workload before the failure of its predecessor would have taught you something you needed. Teams that reverse it -- mail first, because mail feels important -- reliably discover their backup strategy's fiction during the mail outage, with users watching.

The exit-portability standard

What should stay in the cloud

Review the exceptions list annually, because the exceptions age faster than the exits: managed services that justified themselves in 2024 on burstiness may be outgrown by 2026's steadier load, and edge tiers shrink in value as your audience consolidates. Each review asks one question per exception -- "what would it cost, in money and labor, to own this today?" -- and records the answer even when the decision is to stay. A stayed-in-cloud workload with a priced exit option is a strategy; the same workload without one is a dependency, and dependencies are what this entire guide exists to convert.

Two migrations, with the numbers left in

A Kochi logistics startup moved document AI off managed APIs onto owned GPUs over six weeks: $11K/month to $4.2K/month at steady state, p95 latency down 38%, and -- the number their CFO cared about most -- zero pricing-surprise exposure in the following year's budget. A European nonprofit archive moved off single-provider hosting onto a five-node, three-jurisdiction federation over eight weeks: 99.95% measured availability across the next twelve months, three takedown attempts absorbed without downtime, and two volunteer operators now maintaining it from a published runbook. Both migrations followed the order above; both hit their only unplanned rollback at step two, exactly where cheap rollback lives -- which is the ordering doing its job, quietly.

The labor line: automation is cost control

Track the labor line itself, monthly, in one spreadsheet row: hours spent, hours avoided by automation added that month. It is the only metric that keeps automation investment honest, and it produces the sentence finance stakeholders remember: "we bought back eleven hours in March for the price of one." That sentence, not the savings percentage, is usually what unlocks the budget for the next automation -- which is why the consulting practice leads every self-hosting engagement with the labor audit rather than the hardware list.

Household governance: the co-owners problem

The same artifacts scale to small teams with only vocabulary changes: escrow becomes key ceremony, runbook becomes on-call guide, successor clause becomes bus-factor planning -- the succession discipline described on the contributions page for repositories applies unchanged to the box under the stairs. Sovereignty that depends on one person's memory is not sovereignty; it is a subscription to that person's availability, priced in emergencies.

The risk register: what can go wrong, priced

Your first ninety days

Days 1-30: inventory and cost sheet; stand up the boring substrate (DNS you control, a VPN mesh per the WireGuard mesh tutorial, certificates via DNS-01 automation, backups per the restic guides). Days 31-60: migrate classes one and two; run your first full restore drill and write down what broke. Days 61-90: identity move with dual-running; primary-state pilot for one dataset; publish your portability checklist where future-you will find it during an incident. Ninety days later you are not "off the cloud" -- you are the owner of a functioning alternative, which is the position from which every subsequent decision, including staying, is finally free.

A third datapoint, smaller and quieter than the other two: a two-person design studio that moved mail, files, and invoicing off three SaaS subscriptions onto one 16 GB mini PC and a VPS relay. Steady-state cost fell from $61 to $14 per month including amortization; the migration took eleven weekends spread over four months because they refused to rush the mail cutover; and their reported surprise was not savings but calm -- pricing emails from vendors now arrive as information rather than decisions. That calm, priced honestly against the labor line, is the benefit column the cost sheet cannot show and the one clients quote back to me years later.

How do I justify this to a finance-oriented stakeholder?

Lead with the risk register, not the savings: price the last three industry-wide pricing or deprecation events against your usage, then show the cost sheet with labor included. Savings that appear only after hiding labor destroy credibility; a 25% honest saving plus a priced risk column survives finance review, and sometimes the right answer you both reach is "stay, but with exit clauses".
