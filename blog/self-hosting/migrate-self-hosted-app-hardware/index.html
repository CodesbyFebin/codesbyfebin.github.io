How Do I Migrate a Self-Hosted App to New Hardware Without Downtime?

How to migrate a self-hosted app to new hardware without downtime: blue/green DNS, state sync windows, cutover checklists and the rollback plan you test first.

Preparation: replicate before you plan the minute

Stand up the new box from your config repository -- the bare-metal rebuild drill from the checklist is exactly this migration's dress rehearsal, which is why the drill exists. Then replicate state continuously: ZFS incremental sends on a timer for dataset-backed apps, native streaming (Postgres replication, LiteFS, or dump-plus-tail for small SQLite) for databases, restic-then-rsync for file trees. Watch convergence: when incremental lag holds under a minute, the cutover window shrinks to the time it takes to flip one pointer, and "downtime" becomes "one DNS TTL" or less with a proxy swap.

Cutover: the fifteen-minute choreography

Three rules make the choreography trustworthy. Verification happens from outside the LAN (a VPS probe or the federation gateway), because inside views lie about DNS and TLS exactly when they matter. The old box enters standby with state intact and services stopped -- rollback is then a proxy flip backward, not a restore, which is the difference between a five-minute recovery and a weekend. And the freeze window is announced and measured: users forgive fifteen labeled minutes; they never forgive forty unlabeled ones, a lesson every operator learns precisely once.

Soak, then decommission: the week after

Seven days of soak with the old box warm: monitoring compares error rates, latency percentiles, and business signals (sync clients reconnecting, webhooks succeeding) between eras; any anomaly flips backward without ceremony. Decommission only after soak closes: wipe per policy, keep the config-repo entry marked retired with date and successor (the orphan-pruning habit from the edge changelog, applied to hardware), and update the inventory graph so incidents in six months navigate correctly. The retired-machine entry matters more than it feels: half of "why does this hostname resolve?" archaeology ends at a decommissioned box nobody documented, and your future on-call deserves better than a ghost.

The traps that make migrations stories

  • Cron and timers left armed on the old box: a backup timer firing post-cutover writes stale state offsite; disarm before standby, document the arming order for rollback.
  • Hardcoded IPs and localhost assumptions: grep configs and client SDKs for the old address; mesh DNS names pay for themselves exactly here.
  • Certificate scope surprises: names bound to old box paths (ACME account files, webroots) must migrate or re-issue pre-cutover, per the DNS-01 guide's migration notes.
  • State outside the dataset: crontabs, /etc edits made live (the sin the checklist forbids), USB dongles, license files in home directories -- the inventory audit catches them; memory does not.

Every trap above has cost me or a mentee a evening; the checklist rows they became are why this cluster's migrations now read as procedures. The Decentralized.Host rollout machinery is this choreography industrialized -- manifests, health-weighted flips, automatic rollback -- and reading it top-down after a manual migration is the fastest way to understand why federations sleep through cutover nights that homelabs spend awake.

Can I skip the freeze window entirely?

For read-mostly apps with convergent sync (Syncthing-style CRDTs), yes -- cut over per-client and let convergence heal. For single-writer databases, the freeze is the honesty: multi-writer across old and new without engine-level replication is how datasets fork, and forks discovered post-cutover are merges, which are incidents wearing academic vocabulary. Keep the freeze; fifteen minutes buys a linear history.

What if replication lag never converges?

Churn exceeds your pipe or your send cadence: raise frequency to continuous (pipeviewer-watched), exclude churn-heavy caches from the replicated set (regenerate post-cutover), or schedule the cutover into the workload's quiet window and freeze writers longer. Lag that refuses all three indicates the dataset includes something regenerable you are treating as sacred -- audit the dataset boundaries; migrations are excellent audits.

Is migrating a good time to upgrade versions?

No: change one variable per window. Migrate first, soak, then upgrade in the monthly window with its own rollback path -- combining them conflates failure causes at the exact moment you need them separable, and turns a fifteen-minute choreography into an archaeology dig with users watching, which is the definition of a story you will tell for years for the wrong reasons.
