How Do I Auto-Renew Certificates Behind Carrier-Grade NAT?

How to auto-renew certificates behind carrier-grade NAT: DNS-01 automation, provider APIs, staging tests and the monitoring that catches a failed renewal early.

Why the easy path is closed

HTTP-01 requires the CA to reach port 80 on the validated hostname. CGNAT means your router's WAN address is shared and unmappable; port forwarding cannot help what the provider's NAT table does not deliver. DNS-01 sidesteps reachability entirely by asking a question you can answer from anywhere with API credentials: "can you edit this zone?" The trade -- a machine identity over your DNS -- is exactly why the token-scoping table in the DNS-01 guide is the center of that article and the first section any audit should read.

The automation stack that survives reboots

Three settings do the quiet work: Persistent=true (missed windows fire after outages), randomized delay (you are not the CA's thundering herd), and a reload command that is idempotent and verified (systemctl reload caddy plus a post-check comparing served versus on-disk fingerprints). The failure mode this prevents is the 30-day-delayed outage: renewal succeeds, web server keeps the old chain in memory, and users discover your cron in a browser warning.

Propagation polling, bounded and external

After writing the TXT, poll an external resolver (not your provider's nameservers, not your LAN cache) every ten seconds with a three-minute budget; abort loudly on timeout so the journal shows "propagation stall" instead of an opaque CA failure. Split-horizon setups make this non-optional: your LAN resolver answering from cache while the CA sees nothing is a classic self-inflicted mystery. If your provider's propagation is chronically slow, the CNAME trick -- _acme-challenge in the slow zone pointing into a fast, tightly-scoped zone -- buys speed and a smaller token surface simultaneously, and it is the single highest-leverage DNS change in this whole stack.

Monitoring: alert on absence, not presence

Certificate incidents announce themselves to users first unless you invert the alerting: emit a heartbeat metric on every successful renewal and page when it is missing (renewal cadence plus grace), plus a daily external check of served-chain expiry per hostname via your monitoring box. Two alerts, zero noise, full coverage of the failure classes: renewal crash, propagation stall, reload miss, and CA-side outage. Pair with the substrate monitoring from the exit guide so cert heartbeats live beside backup heartbeats -- the two signals whose silence predicts your worst weekends. For what to do when the server also lacks any public path for serving, the exposure guide completes the picture: tunnels carry traffic while DNS-01 carries trust.

My provider has no API. Am I stuck?

Three escapes: move DNS to a provider with zone-scoped APIs (cheapest long-term fix), run your own authoritative server with an API once your substrate habits are proven (sovereignty-maximal, ops-heavy), or use a CNAME delegation to a friend-or-VPS zone you control for _acme-challenge only. The third is the pragmatic bridge I deploy most: your registrar stays, your automation gets a scoped playground, and the blast radius of any token is one subdomain tree.

How early should renewal trigger?

Thirty days before expiry for 90-day certs: enough runway for one failed attempt plus a propagation stall plus a human weekend, without renewing so early that you churn certificates and confuse pinning clients. If any client pins keys (it shouldn't, without a rotation story), coordinate windows explicitly -- pinning plus auto-renewal is a scheduled outage with extra steps.

Wildcard via DNS-01: any hidden costs?

Two: wildcards cannot validate via HTTP-01 ever (so your fallback path disappears -- DNS automation becomes single point of failure, hence the heartbeat alerts), and a wildcard in the wrong place over-exposes subdomain sprawl (every typo subdomain becomes servable with valid TLS, which phishers of your own domain will appreciate). Issue wildcards only for zones whose entire subtree you actually serve; otherwise enumerate names and keep the blast radius contractual.
