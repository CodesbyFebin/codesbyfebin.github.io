How Can I Expose a Self-Hosted App Safely Without a VPN?

How to expose a self-hosted app safely without a full VPN: single-service tunnels, auth proxies, rate limits, audit logs and the ports you should never open.

Threat model in four sentences

An exposed service is scanned within minutes of DNS propagation, by fleets that fingerprint banners and probe default paths. Your realistic adversaries are opportunistic bots (credential stuffing, known-CVE hits), targeted nuisance (someone with a grudge and a script), and -- only for high-value targets -- patient actors. Every control below is sized to the first two, because they account for essentially all homelab traffic; the third is addressed by not hosting high-value targets on infrastructure you defend alone. The asset you protect is not the app but the pivot: one compromised container becomes the whole LAN without network segmentation, which is why exposure decisions are network decisions.

Three doors, ranked

  1. Outbound tunnel (tunnel agent or mesh funnel): zero inbound ports, edge TLS, provider WAF optional; trade is a decrypting intermediary or identity plane in your path -- acceptable for most personal apps, documented in the no-static-IP guide.
  2. VPS reverse proxy over your mesh: one public socket on hardware you patch, TLS via your own DNS-01 wildcards, origin bound to mesh IPs only; best control-to-effort ratio for sensitive services.
  3. Direct port forward: last resort, and only with: auth in front (not the app's built-in login alone), rate limiting at the router or proxy, allowlists where geography permits, and logging shipped off-box. Each forwarded port is a permanent interview with the internet; schedule it like one.

The pre-exposure checklist (run it, then scan yourself)

  • Origin binds 127.0.0.1 or mesh IP -- verify with ss -tlnp, not with belief.
  • AuthN in front: identity-aware proxy or at minimum strong SSO; app-local logins are the second factor of last resort.
  • Rate limits and body limits at the edge; default-deny ingress rules after your explicit hosts.
  • TLS only, HSTS on, ciphers managed by your proxy; no plaintext fallback "for testing".
  • Logs shipped off-box with retention; alert on 4xx storms and auth failures per minute.
  • External verification: nmap from a VPS and a public scanner; the only open ports are the ones you named.
  • Rollback plan: the tunnel/proxy can be disabled in one command, rehearsed once.

The self-scan step catches the classic miss -- a service that dual-binds 0.0.0.0 "temporarily" -- and converts exposure from an intention into a verified state, the same discipline the exit guide applies to migrations: unverified plans are folklore with checkboxes.

The never-open list

Some sockets have no business facing the internet at any security maturity level: database ports (clients reach them through your app or mesh, period), admin planes (router UI, IPMI/BMC, Proxmox console -- the single most-scanned management surface on home ranges), message brokers and RPC endpoints without auth designed for public networks, and anything speaking a protocol whose auth you cannot explain in one sentence. When a workflow seems to require one -- remote database admin, say -- the answer is a mesh session or a bastion with recorded sessions, not a port forward with a strong password; passwords are the lock, segmentation is the wall, and walls are what survive a stolen key. The mesh tutorial's AllowedIPs-as-policy table is the wall-drawing tool this list assumes.

Is Cloudflare's free tier safe enough for a personal app?

For typical personal apps, yes with eyes open: you gain WAF and DDoS absorption but the edge terminates TLS, so plaintext exists at their boundary -- fine for a blog or photo gallery, wrong for health or financial data under your threat model. The sovereignty-consistent upgrade is your own VPS proxy with your own certificates; many homes run both, tunnel for public content, proxy for sensitive.

Do I still need a firewall if I use tunnels?

Yes: default-deny inbound at the router remains your baseline, because tunnels remove the need for forwards but not for the discipline -- a forgotten forward from a previous experiment is exactly what audits find. Additionally firewall egress per host where possible; a compromised container that cannot phone home turns most incidents into non-events, which is cheapest insurance in this entire guide.

How do I expose a service to one friend without a public URL?

Mesh invitation: Tailscale or your WireGuard mesh grants their device an address, and the service stays LAN-bound -- zero public surface, identity-bound access, and revocation is deleting a peer. For non-technical friends, a tunnel with an allowlisted identity (email-based access policies) is the next smallest door; public URLs are the largest and should feel like the last resort they are.
