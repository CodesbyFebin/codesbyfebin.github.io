Stacking economics: three boxes, not twelve

Running twelve projects on twelve boxes is a space heater; the art is bin-packing by profile: Box A (quiet tier): DNS, VPN, mesh, automation -- 6-8 W, 8 GB, fanless if possible, UPS-mandatory (it is the estate's nervous system). Box B (data tier): backup target, photos, media, git -- disks and RAM here, 16 GB, tolerate fan noise in a closet. Box C (compute tier): CI, inference, monitoring -- bursty loads, 32 GB, schedulable overnight. Measured combined idle: 31 W; combined replacement value in subscriptions: four figures annually, per the cost sheets in the exit guide. The tiering also isolates failure domains: Box C may die mid-batch without touching photos or the doorbell, which is exactly the segmentation the exposure guide preaches at network layer, repeated at hardware layer.

Buying and commissioning notes

Used office mini PCs (1L corporate boxes, ex-lease) deliver the best watt-per-euro in this entire hobby: 8th-12th gen cores, ECC-less but reliable, NVMe slots, and 10 W idles after undervolting folklore is skipped entirely (they ship conservative). Commissioning follows the checklist rows: fresh install from your config repo (NixOS or Debian per the comparison), mesh address and DNS name assigned before services, monitoring agent first so the box is observable before it is useful, and a burn-in week running memtest plus disk scrub cycles before any real data lands. The burn-in week is the project equivalent of the federation's probation period: hardware lies early and honestly if you give it a microphone, and a fan or DIMM that will fail in month three usually confesses in day four.

Noise, heat, and the furniture problem

The three killers of mini-PC deployments are not software: enclosed media cabinets (thermal throttling per the mesh-on-minis Q&A's pilot lesson), bedroom-adjacent fan noise (choose fanless for the quiet tier; measure dB at one meter before arguing with family), and the cable tangle that makes maintenance scary (label both ends, service loop per box, photograph before closing any panel). Households that treat placement as architecture report higher maintenance-window adherence -- the box you can reach and hear is the box you actually maintain, and maintenance, not hardware, is what every uptime story in this cluster finally reduces to.

One procurement footnote that saves real money: buy two of whichever box becomes your data tier, commissioning the second as a cold spare rather than a second server -- the spare sleeps in the escrow shelf with your config already evaluated against it during one annual drill, turning hardware death from a migration event into a swap-and-boot afternoon. The spare costs less than one month of the subscriptions the stack replaces, and its existence converts every other risk row in this list from threat into procedure, which is the psychological dividend runbooks quietly depend on and hardware people chronically underestimate. Label the spare, date its last drill, and keep its firmware matched to the primary -- a spare that diverges is a surprise with a dust cover, and surprises are precisely what the spare existed to eliminate.
