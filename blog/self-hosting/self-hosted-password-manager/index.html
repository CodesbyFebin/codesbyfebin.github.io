Deployment: small, proxied, monitored

SIGNUPS_ALLOWED=false after creating your household accounts removes the discovery-to-spam pipeline entirely; new members join by invitation through the admin console, which itself is bound to /admin and should be reachable only via your mesh or an identity-aware proxy per the exposure guide. Back the container's /data volume with the restic pipeline from this cluster -- the vault database is small, dedupes beautifully, and is the one dataset where restore drills deserve monthly rather than quarterly cadence, because the day you need it is by definition the day everything else is also on fire.

Threat model: what self-hosting changes and doesn't

  • Removed: third-party breach headlines as your incident timeline; vendor policy changes on data retention; subscription as a single point of family lockout.
  • Unchanged: client-side cryptography is the same code Bitwarden ships -- your master password and device hygiene remain the vault; phishing remains the top real-world attack on any password manager.
  • Added: availability is your job (UPS, backups, renewal of the edge cert in front of it), and patching cadence is your policy -- set it monthly with security releases jumping the queue.

The honest summary: you trade diffuse vendor risk for concentrated personal responsibility, exactly the exchange the worth-it analysis prices across services -- and for password managers specifically, the ciphertext-at-rest property makes the personal side unusually winnable, because a compromised server yields encrypted blobs whose keys live only in your household's heads and hardware keys.

Passkeys and 2FA: the layer that saves the vault

Enroll WebAuthn keys (hardware tokens or platform authenticators) for every household admin before inviting anyone else: credential stuffing against your edge is commodity traffic, and a passkey-gated admin console turns it into noise. Per-item practices matter more than server choices for daily safety: unique generated passwords everywhere (the manager's generator, 20+ chars), TOTP inside the vault only for legacy services that lack passkeys, and one documented emergency-access pair (a trusted human with time-delayed emergency access) so the escrow envelope has a human twin. Test emergency access annually like any other restore: an untested delegation is a rumor with a settings page.

Exports, migration, and exit portability

Quarterly, produce an encrypted export (.zip or encrypted JSON) into the escrow envelope and one offsite target; label it with date and vault version. This is the exit-portability clause from the exit guide applied at item granularity: migration to any future manager (self-hosted or otherwise) becomes an import of a standard format rather than a rescue operation, and the envelope covers the catastrophic tier where the server, the backups, and the house are one incident. Keep the export script in your config repository beside the deployment, because a documented export that lives only in someone's memory is a future lockout wearing a backup's costume -- the same lesson the runbook rows teach across this entire cluster.

Label exports with date, vault version, and item count, and verify the count on restore: a silently partial export -- one interrupted attachment, one skipped collection -- is indistinguishable from a complete one until the Sunday it isn't, and a checksummed manifest beside each archive turns that class of surprise into a one-line comparison at drill time.

Is storing the whole family vault on one box risky?

Concentrated, not risky-by-itself: the ciphertext property means the box holds locked boxes, and your drills plus escrow cover availability while WebAuthn covers access. The genuinely risky family pattern is shared master passwords instead of per-member accounts with org-sharing -- per-member accounts keep revocation (a lost phone, a departed member) a settings change instead of a household rekey weekend.

What breaks first after a year of operation?

One household habit worth stealing regardless of manager: a printed card in the escrow envelope with the vault URL, the export location, and the emergency-access human's phone number -- because the failure mode that actually strands families is not server death but information asymmetry at the worst possible hour, and paper remains the only medium with 100% client compatibility. Review the card annually beside the export -- same envelope, same quarter, same ten minutes as the restore drill -- because a vault whose recovery path is untested is not a vault but a very organized way of losing everything at once, and the entire thesis of this cluster is that organized and verified are different words for a reason: only one of them has ever saved anybody on a bad Sunday.
