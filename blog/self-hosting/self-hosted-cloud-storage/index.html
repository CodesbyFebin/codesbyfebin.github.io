The labor column is the decision: every stack works, and every stack demands a different weekly attention shape. Nextcloud's app ecosystem means quarterly upgrade testing (apps break majors); Seafile's library model means onboarding conversations ("which library holds what"); Syncthing's decentralization means device inventory discipline (a forgotten phone keeps syncing deleted shares). Match the labor to your temperament, not to feature checklists -- abandoned storage stacks are uniformly the ones whose labor shape mismatched their owner's week, per the cohort observations in the worth-it analysis.

Deployment constants across all three

  • Storage beneath: ZFS or RAID-backed volumes with scrub schedules; storage stacks amplify disk lies into sync conflicts, so the substrate drills from the ZFS guide apply unchanged.
  • TLS at your edge: the proxy guide's shape -- origins loopback-bound, wildcards via DNS-01, HSTS on after the first clean month.
  • Backups beside, not inside: sync is not backup (deletion syncs instantly); restic from the storage volume to offsite per the automation guide, with the canary directory proving freshness.
  • Quotas and retention: per-user quotas from day one (storage without quotas is a DoS by family photo), and trash retention policies written down before the first "where did it go" ticket.

Migrating in without losing a weekend

Order matters: seed from existing cloud downloads (not sync clients fighting each other), verify counts and checksums at rest before inviting devices, then onboard clients one household member at a time with the old service read-only -- parallel writable syncs of the same tree are how conflict-file archaeology enters your family legend. Keep the old subscription alive one billing cycle as rollback, then cancel with the exit-portability checklist signed off per the exit guide: open formats out (both suites export standards), IaC in repository, restore drilled once before the cancellation click. The cancellation click, incidentally, is the moment households report the feeling the cost sheet cannot price -- the subscription email that arrives next quarter as news instead of invoice, which is sovereignty's quiet dividend in storage as in everything else this cluster builds.

One closing measurement habit: publish your cost-per-gigabyte-year quarterly (hardware amortization plus power plus your labor hours valued) beside the consumer plan's price for the same volume. Households are consistently surprised in both directions -- some discover they run cheaper than the subscription, others discover they pay a premium for ownership -- and both discoveries are useful, because the premium buyers then know exactly what they are purchasing (privacy, egress freedom, exit options) and can defend the line item in any household or finance conversation, which is the difference between a hobby that survives budget reviews and one that does not.

Whichever stack you choose, commission it with the same four artifacts every other service in this cluster receives: a runbook page (restart, restore-one-file, restore-everything), a heartbeat in monitoring (sync lag plus quota headroom), a backup row in the offsite chain, and a sunset review date -- storage stacks outlive their owners' enthusiasm more often than any other homelab component, and the artifacts are what carry them across the gap between enthusiasm and habit.

What breaks first at family scale?

Client-side: a phone syncing camera rolls over cellular mid-vacation (quota plus egress surprises), and a laptop holding deleted shares in trash forever (retention policy nobody configured). Server-side: PHP/runtime memory limits under bulk uploads for suites, and database vacuum neglect over year two. All four are configuration, not product, failures -- which is the storage stack's version of this cluster's recurring finding: the cryptography and the software are reliable; the plumbing is the practice.
