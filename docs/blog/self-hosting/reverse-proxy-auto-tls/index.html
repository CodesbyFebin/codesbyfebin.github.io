Caddy: the zero-config path, done properly

Caddy's magic is ACME wired into the server lifecycle: certificates obtain at first request, renew before expiry, and reload without downtime. The discipline around the magic is what production adds: origins bound to localhost only (verify with ss -tlnp, the check from the exposure guide), explicit HSTS once you are sure (it is a one-way door per browser), and the DNS-01 plugin configured with the scoped tokens the DNS-01 guide treats as crown jewels. On-demand TLS for multi-tenant edges exists but needs a permission endpoint -- never enable it open to arbitrary hostnames, or your proxy becomes the internet's certificate laundry.

Nginx plus ACME clients: the explicit-control path

Nginx gives you per-directive control and decades of operational literature; the cost is owning renewal yourself. The shape that ages well: certbot or acme.sh with webroot or DNS-01 plugins, systemd timers with Persistent=true (the same persistence argument as backups and certs elsewhere in this cluster), a reload -- not restart -- on renewal so in-flight requests drain, and a config test (nginx -t) inside the renewal hook so a bad template fails the renewal loudly instead of the reload silently. Snippet discipline matters more than directives: one ssl-hardening.conf and one headers.conf included by every server block, versioned in your config repository, because forty copy-pasted server blocks with drifted cipher lists are how "we have TLS" becomes "we have forty TLS configurations".

Headers, protocols, and the edge's real job

  • HSTS after your first clean month; preload only for domains you accept owning forever.
  • HTTP/2 and HTTP/3: on by default in Caddy, one directive in modern Nginx; QUIC needs UDP 443 open and pays off on lossy mobile paths.
  • Compression at the edge for text types only; never compress already-compressed media, and mind BREACH-class thinking on authenticated responses.
  • Client-body limits at the proxy (the same default-body-limit reasoning as the axum tutorial), raised per-route with intent.
  • Logging with request ids propagated to origins via headers, so traces span the edge instead of dying at it.

Monitoring the terminator

Watch four things at the edge: certificate expiry per name (external checks, not internal state), TLS handshake errors by version (sudden SNI or protocol anomalies precede client-ecosystem shifts), 5xx ratio per origin (the edge sees origin death before users do), and config-reload success (a failed reload that leaves an old worker fleet serving is a time bomb with a log line). Alert on silence as elsewhere in this cluster: the renewal heartbeat and the reload heartbeat are the two metrics whose absence means your automation died politely. With those green, the edge disappears from your attention entirely -- the correct experience of good termination is never thinking about termination, while the behind-NAT renewal Q&A keeps the certificate half honest in the hardest networks.

Keep one more artifact beside the dashboards: a dated edge-config changelog entry per server block added or removed. Edges accumulate orphan sites the way gardens accumulate weeds, and a quarterly review that deletes dead server blocks shrinks your certificate blast radius and your confusion in the same ten minutes. Ten minutes a quarter, four times a year, forever: the cheapest insurance in this article, and the only policy whose payout you can read in a single diff.

Caddy or Nginx for a homelab in 2026?

Caddy, unless you need a directive Nginx has and Caddy lacks (rare) or your team already speaks fluent Nginx (real). The operational surface you avoid -- renewal hooks, reload choreography, snippet drift -- is exactly the surface where homelabs leak weekends; spend the saved time on restore drills, which protect more than any edge choice.

Should the proxy also be the WAF?

As a first layer, yes-ish: rate limits, body caps, and geo-allowlists at the edge stop commodity noise cheaply. As a security boundary, no: an edge WAF is a smoke detector, not a firewall -- authentication, authorization, and input validation belong in the app or an identity-aware proxy in front of it. Teams that treat ModSecurity-class rules as their security plan maintain rules; teams that treat them as noise-reduction maintain services.

How do I terminate TLS for non-HTTP protocols?

TLS passthrough (SNI routing without termination) for protocols you cannot parse, or the proxy's stream/TCP mode (Nginx stream, Caddy layer4) for termination with client-cert auth where the protocol supports it. Database and broker exposure should still ride the mesh rather than any public edge per the never-open list; passthrough exists for the exceptions that survive that list's review, documented per port like every other policy decision here.
