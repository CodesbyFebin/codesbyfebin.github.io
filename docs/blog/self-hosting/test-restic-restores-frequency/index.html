How Often Should I Test Restic Restores, and How?

How often to test restic restores: a risk-based schedule, automated partial-restore checks in CI, and the quarterly full-drill procedure that keeps backups honest.

The risk math behind the cadence

Backups fail in three hidden ways: writes that never landed (misconfigured paths, silent exclusions), repositories that corrupted between checks, and restores that break for reasons the backup never exercised (missing keys, changed hostnames, new encryption defaults). Each class has a detection latency you can shrink only by restoring: restic check verifies repository integrity but not that your data is in it; only a restore answers that. Price it: if your dataset's change rate makes a week of loss painful, weekly partials bound the surprise to seven days of drift; quarterly fulls bound the "restore procedure itself is broken" surprise to one quarter, which matches how often hosts, tools, and humans change enough to break procedures.

Weekly: automated partial restores in CI

The canary directory is the trick that makes partials meaningful: a small tree updated daily with known content, so "the restore worked" includes "the newest bytes are the right bytes". Random paths catch exclusion-config rot (someone added --exclude '*.db' two years ago and nobody remembers why). Both results feed one heartbeat metric; missing heartbeats page you, because a verification job that died silently is indistinguishable from a backup that stopped.

Quarterly: the full-restore drill, rehearsed like a fire drill

Ad-hoc triggers: restore immediately after these events

  • Any change to backup configuration, storage, or network path.
  • Ransomware or intrusion suspicion anywhere on the source host.
  • Repository migration, prune-policy change, or restic major-version upgrade.
  • Hardware replacement on the backup target (disks lie quietly first).

Each trigger gets a partial restore within the hour, not the week: the question "did the change break backups?" has a five-minute answer and a three-month answer, and only one of them is acceptable right after a change. Pair with offsite copies per the cluster's offsite-backup guidance once it ships; a drill that restores from the same box that holds the source proves less than you think during a disk event.

Does restic check replace restore testing?

No -- they answer different questions. restic check (and --read-data) verifies repository structures and pack integrity; a restore verifies that your files, paths, permissions, and retention actually produce usable data on a different host. Run check weekly as cheap insurance and restores as the only real proof; teams that substitute one for the other discover the gap during the incident.

How long should a quarterly drill be allowed to take?

As long as your RTO promises, plus 20% margin -- and if the drill exceeds that, the finding is architectural (faster target, parallel streams, narrower retention) rather than procedural. Logging drill duration every quarter builds the trend line that turns "restores feel slow" into a capacity decision with numbers attached.

Should drills restore to production or scratch space?

Scratch, always, except in a real incident: drills must never mutate production state or compete with production IO. The one exception that earns its risk is an annual "cold start" exercise -- rebuild an entire service on bare hardware from repo plus backups -- because that drill tests the thing partials cannot: whether your runbook can recreate a machine from nothing, which is the actual shape of the worst day.
