Why DNS-01 exists: the validations HTTP can't do

HTTP-01 asks "can you serve a file at this domain?" -- useless when the domain points at a CGNAT'd home line or when the certificate is a wildcard (Let's Encrypt permits wildcards only via DNS-01). DNS-01 asks "can you edit this zone?" -- a question NAT cannot block and wildcards cannot complicate. The trade is real: you grant a machine identity over your DNS, which is why token scoping and audit logging are not optional appendices to this guide but its center of gravity. Everything else is plumbing; the token is the crown.

Where a provider cannot scope to a zone, scope the domain: register the self-hosting identity on its own domain with its own registrar account, so a leaked token burns one zone instead of your portfolio. Store tokens in your secrets manager (never in the renewal script), rotate annually, and log every use -- the audit trail doubles as renewal monitoring, since a token that stops working shows up as a missing log line before it shows up as an expired certificate.

Propagation waits: bounded, observable, patient

DNS is eventually consistent by design; your renewal job must be eventually patient. The pattern that never wakes me: after writing the TXT, poll an external resolver (not the provider's own nameservers) every 10 s with a 180 s budget; on timeout, abort loudly rather than letting the CA time out opaquely. Multi-vantage checks matter for split-horizon zones: your LAN resolver may answer from cache while the CA's validators see nothing. If your provider's propagation is routinely slow, pre-create a long-TTL _acme-challenge CNAME to a fast-scoped zone -- the indirection buys you both speed and a token surface you actually control.

Renewal timers that survive reboots, redeploys, and you

Three rules finish the job: renew at 30 days remaining (not 24 hours -- propagation plus one failed attempt needs runway); make the reload command idempotent and tested (a renewal that fetches a cert but never reloads the web server is a 30-day-delayed outage); and alert on the absence of success -- a heartbeat metric per domain into whatever monitoring the exit guide's substrate month stood up, because certificate incidents announce themselves in browser chrome, politely, to your users first.

Staging first: the rehearsal that costs nothing

Run the entire chain -- issue, propagate, install, reload, verify with openssl s_client -- against the CA's staging environment until it passes twice, then flip one flag. Staging rate limits are generous and its certs are untrusted by design, which is exactly the property you want while a cron typo is still possible. The same discipline applies after any provider API change: staging catches their breaking changes during your office hours. For what to do when the server has no inbound path at all, pair this guide with hosting without a static IP; for wildcard-serving patterns once certs exist, the federation's edge layer in Decentralized.Host documents its DNS-01 automation in the same shape.

Can I use DNS-01 with a self-hosted DNS server instead of a provider?

Yes, and it is the sovereignty-maximal setup: run your own authoritative server (PowerDNS or Knot) with a scoped API, and DNS-01 becomes pure local automation with zero third-party token. The cost is that your DNS server becomes internet-reachable infrastructure -- monitoring, TSIG/API auth, and a secondary somewhere else's network -- so adopt it after, not before, your substrate habits are proven.

Why did renewal succeed but my browser still show the old cert?

Almost always a missing or failed reload step: the cert files changed on disk while the serving process kept the old chain in memory. Verify with openssl s_client against the port, not with the file mtime; then fix the reload command (some servers need restart, not reload, for TLS material) and add a post-renewal check that fails loudly when served and on-disk fingerprints disagree.

Are 90-day certificates going away in favor of shorter ones?

The direction is shorter, not longer: industry roadmaps point toward 45-day and eventually ~10-day public certs, which turns renewal from a quarterly worry into pure automation. Every hour spent making your DNS-01 chain boring, observed, and staging-tested now is future-proofing against that timeline -- manual renewal workflows are already dead; they just haven't stopped moving yet.
