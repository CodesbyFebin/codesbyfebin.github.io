Why Is My WireGuard Handshake Failing Behind Double NAT?

Why WireGuard handshakes fail behind double NAT: endpoint fixation, keepalives, CGNAT symmetry and the relay patterns that restore connectivity without open ports.

What a handshake needs, mechanically

WireGuard is silent until traffic flows: a peer sends a handshake initiation to its configured Endpoint, the other side replies, and a session key is established. Behind ordinary (cone) NAT this works because the outgoing packet opens a pinhole the reply can reuse. Behind symmetric NAT -- most CGNAT, many router defaults -- the pinhole is per-destination, and since both peers initiate toward addresses that are not the other's real socket, the two holes never align. The protocol is fine; the address geometry is impossible. Everything below is a way of giving at least one side a predictable address.

Diagnose in sixty seconds

Two patterns separate cleanly. If the reachable peer sees initiation packets arriving from varying source ports, symmetric NAT is rewriting them -- geometry problem, keepalive alone will not fix it. If it sees nothing at all, check the simpler culprits first: firewall rules on the WAN router, wrong Endpoint port, ISP blocking UDP 51820 (rare but real), or clock skew beyond WireGuard's timestamp tolerance, which produces the distinctive "handshake replied but never established" log rhythm.

The three fixes, cheapest first

  1. Keepalives plus one reachable anchor. If at least one peer has a stable address (VPS, IPv6, port-forwarded home), set PersistentKeepalive = 25 on the trapped peers toward it; the warm pinhole lets the anchor relay introductions or simply serve as hub. Cheapest, fixes most homes.
  2. Relay through the hub. Route trapped-to-trapped traffic via the anchor by giving the hub AllowedIPs = 10.66.0.0/24 and enabling forwarding -- the hybrid topology in the mesh tutorial, where this case is exactly why the hub exists.
  3. Change the geometry. IPv6 end-to-end (many CGNATs are IPv4-only), a $3 VPS as one peer's home, or a router that supports full-cone mode. Most durable, most effort; do it for nodes that matter at 3 a.m.

After any fix, verify with wg show handshakes advancing every few minutes and bidirectional transfer counters; then add a monitoring alert on handshake age per peer, because NAT behavior drifts with router firmware updates and ISP reconfigurations -- the mesh that worked in March can exile a node in April without anyone touching a config, and the kerala-mesh pilot learned that lesson one firmware-update Tuesday.

Preventing the class, not the instance

Record each peer's NAT class in your topology file at commissioning time (test with a STUN utility once; symmetric vs cone is stable per router config). Generate keepalive and relay settings from that record instead of troubleshooting per node, and re-run the classification in your quarterly maintenance window alongside the restore drills the cluster preaches elsewhere. Field truth from flood-prone deployments: power-cycle tests double as NAT tests, because routers that reboot into different defaults are how symmetric NAT arrives unannounced.

Does PersistentKeepalone fix double NAT by itself?

No. Keepalives keep a pinhole warm toward the configured Endpoint; when both ends are behind symmetric NAT, each keepalive warms a hole the other side's packets still cannot use, because source ports differ per destination. Keepalives fix cone-NAT roaming and idle-timeout cases; double NAT needs an anchor, a relay, or a geometry change as listed above.

Can two CGNAT peers connect over IPv4 at all?

Only with help: a relay node with a public address (the hub pattern), or hole-punching coordination protocols that WireGuard deliberately does not implement. Accept the relay: one extra hop through your own VPS preserves the mesh's security properties while restoring reachability, and the latency cost for home traffic is milliseconds nobody feels.

Is UDP blocking by the ISP a real possibility?

Occasionally, especially on mobile-carrier CGNAT and some satellite links that shape or drop long UDP flows. Test by moving the WireGuard port to 443/UDP or tunneling over TCP as a diagnostic only; if that "fixes" it, you have your answer and a decision to make -- permanent TCP tunneling costs performance, so most operators switch that peer to a relay path instead.
