Topology choice: full mesh, hub, or relay -- decide by failure mode

Keys and distribution: the only secret ceremony you need

Distribution discipline is the whole security story: private keys are generated on-node and never transmitted; the generator host collects public keys and emits configs. If your process ever copies a private key through chat or email, rotate it -- the ceremony's value is that compromise requires physical node access, and breaking that property silently is how meshes become decorations.

NAT traversal and MTU: the two knobs behind 90% of mesh tickets

WireGuard tunnels through NAT by hole-punching from both ends; when one side sits behind symmetric NAT (most CGNAT), the handshake never completes until traffic flows from the reachable side first -- which is why PersistentKeepalive = 25 on the trapped peers is not optional tuning, it is the feature. Verify with wg show: a peer with no latest handshake after keepalives is your double-NAT exile, candidate for the relay path (the Q&A on handshakes failing behind double NAT walks the diagnosis end to end). MTU comes next: path-MTU discovery dies inside tunnels more often than outside them; set MTU = 1420 as the default and drop to 1360 on PPPoE or satellite legs. The signature of an MTU bug is SSH banners that hang mid-transfer while pings fly -- large packets black-holed, small ones fine.

Generator plus CI: configs that cannot drift

Hand-edited WireGuard configs rot exactly like hand-edited firewalls: one emergency change at a time, until no diagram matches reality. The generator keeps a single source of truth (topology YAML plus pubkey directory) and emits every node's file; the CI diff makes staleness a merge blocker instead of a 2 a.m. mystery. This is the same change-management philosophy the Decentralized.Host manifest chain applies to deploys -- signed, append-only, regenerable -- scaled down to a home mesh's budget.

Field notes from the pilot

Three lessons from a year of monsoon-season mesh operation. Power beats protocol: nodes on un-UPS'd lines produced more outage minutes than any cryptographic or routing issue, and a $40 UPS per node was the highest-ROI "networking" purchase of the pilot. Roaming clients want DNS, not memory: give mesh addresses names (nas.mesh, pi4.mesh) via a tiny DNS on the hub, because humans fail at remembering 10.66.0.7 exactly when stressed. And rehearse peer loss: unplug the hub monthly during maintenance windows; the hybrid topology's fallback paths are only real if someone has watched them engage. For exposing services over the mesh without opening ports, pair with the tunnel patterns in hosting without a static IP, and keep the exit-plan context from the exit-the-cloud guide -- a mesh is substrate, and substrate is what makes every later migration cheap.

How many peers can one hub realistically relay?

Relaying is bandwidth- and CPU-light (crypto is per-endpoint, forwarding is memcpy): a 1 vCPU VPS with 100 Mbps comfortably relays 20-30 active peers at home-office traffic levels. What kills hubs is not throughput but config sprawl and unmonitored failure -- add per-peer traffic alerts before you add your twentieth peer, not after.

Should mesh traffic be re-encrypted by applications?

Yes for anything sensitive: WireGuard secures the path, not the endpoint, and hub-relayed traffic terminates cryptographically at the hub's kernel but not at its operator's curiosity if apps speak plaintext. TLS inside the tunnel is free with your DNS-01 wildcards; treat the mesh as untrusted LAN until every service on it proves otherwise.

Is a mesh worth it for a single home with no VPS?

Marginally: one node behind one NAT needs a relay anyway, so the honest setup is home node plus cheapest VPS as hub -- two peers, ten minutes, and you gain roaming access plus the substrate for everything else in this cluster. The mesh payoff compounds at three-plus nodes; below that, call it what it is: a very good VPN.
