Post-Quantum Proof Systems: Why STARKs Survive Shor's Algorithm

Post-quantum proof systems surveyed: why hash-based STARKs resist Shor's algorithm, where lattice proofs fit, and which parameters future-proof verifiable compute.

The two quantum attacks, sized honestly

Shor's algorithm breaks factoring and discrete logs in polynomial time, killing RSA, ECC, and -- relevant here -- pairing-based and DLOG-based SNARKs whose soundness reduces to those problems. Grover's search quadratically weakens hash and symmetric primitives: a 96-bit hash-security margin behaves like ~48 bits against a large-scale quantum adversary. The asymmetry between those two effects is the whole landscape: one is existential, the other is a margin tax. Hence the standard posture -- migrate away from Shor-exposed assumptions entirely, and pay the Grover tax by doubling security bits where artifact lifetimes demand it, exactly as the forgery Q&A sizes for long-lived proofs.

Where STARKs stand under quantum scrutiny

STARK soundness rests on hash collision resistance (Merkle commitments, Fiat-Shamir challenges) and Reed-Solomon proximity bounds (combinatorial, not number-theoretic). Neither admits a Shor-style attack; the quantum-relevant analyses concern Fiat-Shamir in the quantum random-oracle model and proximity bounds under quantum queries -- active research whose published results remain favorable at standard parameterizations, with citations maintained on the research page. Honest summary: hash-based STARKs lean post-quantum; they are not certified against adversaries nobody can model fully, and any vendor saying otherwise is selling certainty the literature does not contain.

Lattice and hash-based alternatives, briefly

  • Lattice-based SNARKs/zk arguments: post-quantum soundness from module-learning-with-errors style assumptions; larger proofs and younger analyses than STARKs, but actively improving and attractive where SNARK-shaped verification is required.
  • Hash-based signatures for proof envelopes: signing proofs and manifests with Dilithium or SPHINCS+ keeps the attestation chain post-quantum end to end -- a cheap addition many systems skip and later regret.
  • Hybrid compositions: classical plus post-quantum signatures, STARK inner proofs with PQ-signed manifests: belt-and-braces patterns that cost bytes and buy era-independence.

The composition principle repeats from every other layer of this portfolio: keep inner claims verifiable under multiple assumption sets, archive the artifacts each era needs, and let future verifiers choose their own trust -- the same argument the comparison article makes for archival composition, extended here across time rather than across verifier classes.

A migration plan for proof fleets

  1. Inventory by lifetime: classify artifacts by how long they must convince (CI ephemera: years; compliance: decades; archives: effectively forever).
  2. Set bit-margins per class: 96-bit for short-lived, 128-bit for long-lived, accepting the ~12-18% prover cost the field Q&A measures.
  3. Pin and manifest everything: field, security bits, hash families, signature schemes -- future verifiers need the geometry, not guesses.
  4. Re-verify on era boundaries: schedule re-checks of archival proofs as analyses mature; a proof re-verified in 2035 under 2035 assumptions is worth two from 2025.

Notice what the plan does not include: panic, or rip-and-replace. Quantum-relevant computers remain a research-scale threat today; the artifacts at risk are the long-lived ones, and their defense is arithmetic margin plus record-keeping -- the two cheapest commodities in engineering, and the two most often skipped until an audit asks.

Two implementation notes close the loop. First, hash-agility: name the hash family in every manifest and keep verifier code path-agnostic over families, so a future migration becomes a parameter rollout with dual-run windows instead of a repository rewrite -- the versioning discipline from the specifications page applies unchanged to primitives. Second, dual signatures: envelopes signed classically today should carry a post-quantum signature line wherever artifact lifetimes exceed a decade; the extra bytes buy era-independence, and bytes remain the cheap commodity while trust across eras is the scarce one. Neither note requires new mathematics; both require the record-keeping habit that makes mathematics portable across time, which after four clusters of evidence is the single most transferable skill this blog teaches.

Should new systems ship lattice-based proofs today?

Only where the requirement is explicit (government or long-horizon compliance mandates); otherwise hash-based STARKs offer mature tooling, measured performance, and analyses with longer public review. Lattice proof systems are improving quickly -- review annually -- but "post-quantum" in a spec should name assumptions and citations, not vibes, and today the citation depth still favors hashes for production.

Does quantum computing break my existing archived proofs?

Not retroactively for hash-based systems at adequate margins: an adversary must attack the hash assumptions to forge, and Grover halving at 96-bit leaves ~48-bit margins -- thin but non-trivial; at 128-bit, comfortable. Pairing-based archived proofs are the exposed class: plan re-proving or hybrid re-attestation for those artifacts if their claims must still convince post-quantum.
