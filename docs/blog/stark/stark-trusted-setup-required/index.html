Do STARKs Require a Trusted Setup?

Do STARKs need a trusted setup? No — why hash-based soundness removes the ceremony, what randomness remains, and which proof systems still require one.

What a trusted setup actually is

Some proof systems build their verifying key from structured random values ("toxic waste") that must be destroyed after generation: whoever retains them can create proofs of false statements forever. Communities mitigate with multiparty ceremonies where one honest participant suffices -- but the integrity story then lives in ceremony transcripts, audits, and human process, forever. This is not a theoretical worry; it is a permanent operational dependency, and every artifact verified under such a key inherits it, including artifacts meant to outlive the ceremony's participants and even their institutions.

Why STARKs structurally avoid it

STARK soundness rests on two public ingredients: collision-resistant hashes (for Merkle commitments and Fiat-Shamir challenges) and Reed-Solomon proximity bounds (a low-degree polynomial cannot agree with a far-from-low-degree function on many points). Challenges are derived by hashing the proof transcript itself, so no pre-generated randomness exists to leak; the verifier recomputes them from public data. The only "secret" anywhere is the prover's witness, which zero-knowledge blinding protects with random polynomials -- randomness used once, in the open protocol, and never trusted to anyone's deletion habits.

The randomness that does remain, named precisely

  • Fiat-Shamir hashing: challenges come from a hash of the transcript; its security is heuristic in the random-oracle model, and quantum analyses of FS remain active research -- honest writing says "leans post-quantum", as the comparison article insists.
  • Grinding nonce: the prover performs bounded proof-of-work to bind the transcript; the factor is public and asserted at verification, so weakening it is visible, not secret.
  • Blinding polynomials: per-proof randomness for zero-knowledge, generated and discarded by the prover; compromise hurts privacy of that proof, not soundness of the system.

Which systems still need ceremonies

Groth16-family SNARKs (per-circuit setup, smallest proofs) and some universal-setup schemes (powers-of-tau, reusable but still ceremony-born). Modern universal setups reduce per-circuit risk and many ecosystems audit them well; the residual question is lifetime: a universal ceremony's compromise breaks everything keyed to it, which is a larger blast radius than per-circuit waste. If your verifier environment forces a ceremony-based system (on-chain gas budgets usually do), keep the STARK inner proof archived alongside, so future verifiers can re-check the claim under assumptions their era trusts -- composition as insurance, a pattern detailed in the zkVM tutorial's verification section.

And when auditing any vendor's "setup-free" claim, ask which family the verifier belongs to: marketing regularly conflates universal setups with no setup, and the difference is precisely a ceremony -- one reused across circuits instead of per circuit, but a ceremony nonetheless, with participants, transcripts, and a blast radius worth naming in your risk register.

Does "no trusted setup" mean no parameters at all?

No: public parameters remain -- field choice, security bits, grinding factor, query counts -- but they are public configuration, verifiable by inspection and asserted in every proof envelope. The phrase bans secret trust, not configuration discipline; confusing the two is how teams ship weak parameters believing the setup-free property covers them.

Can a prover cheat the grinding nonce?

Grinding raises the cost of finding acceptable transcripts; skipping it weakens soundness by exactly the advertised bits, and verifiers check the nonce against the declared factor. A prover that under-grinds produces proofs honest verifiers reject -- the parameter is enforced at verification, which is the only place enforcement means anything.

Is Fiat-Shamir a hidden trust assumption?

It is a modeled assumption (random oracle), not a trusted party: no human holds anything, and breaks require attacking the hash's structure, not someone's honesty. That distinction -- trust in mathematics versus trust in process -- is the entire content of "no trusted setup", and it is why the answer to this page's question stays a clean no.
