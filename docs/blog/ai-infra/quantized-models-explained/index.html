What quantization actually does

Weights trained as FP16 numbers are clustered into far fewer levels: 4-bit gives 16 levels per block, with per-block scales preserving local magnitude. The model's knowledge survives because training over-parameterized it; the losses concentrate where precision mattered least on average -- which is exactly why averages hide your task's tail. Modern schemes (K-quants, AWQ) protect sensitive channels by measuring importance during calibration, so "4-bit" in 2026 is materially better than "4-bit" in 2023, and any advice older than two years should be re-measured, not repeated.

The practical rule: hardware picks the format family, workload picks the bits. A 8 GB laptop is GGUF Q4 by physics; a 24 GB server running shared inference is AWQ by throughput; FP16 stays in the lab as the measurement baseline against which every quantized claim in your stack gets checked -- including the ones in this article, whose numbers live in the cluster's benchmark notes.

Where quantization meets serving economics

Quantization choices propagate past memory into serving economics. Smaller weights mean more concurrent sessions in the same VRAM, because freed capacity becomes KV headroom: on a 24 GB card, Q4 8B serves roughly triple the interactive sessions of FP16 at equal context, a capacity difference no scheduler tuning can replicate. On CPU boxes the effect inverts pleasingly: narrower integers accelerate the matrix-vector passes that dominate generation (more values per SIMD lane), so Q4 can beat Q8 on tokens-per-second at the same bandwidth -- the rare case where quantization buys speed as well as size, and the reason CPU deployments often settle at Q4 while GPU deployments climb to Q8 for quality.

Power deserves the same ledger: fewer bytes moved per token means fewer watts per answer, and in always-on home deployments the yearly electricity delta between Q8 and Q4 can exceed the hardware amortization line the exit guide's cost sheet prices. When a household asks whether quantization is "worth" a quality point, the complete answer therefore includes the watt-meter and the concurrency column beside the rubric -- three numbers, one decision, all measurable in an evening. That is this cluster's standing rule for every adjective in the space: quality, speed, size, and watts are four projections of one artifact, and choosing honestly means looking at all four before believing anyone's single-number summary, including mine.

Choosing bits: a test protocol, not a vibe

  1. Assemble 50-100 real requests from your own logs or workflows (benchmarks measure someone else's workload).
  2. Score FP16 (or Q8 if FP16 cannot run) as baseline with an automatic metric plus a 1-5 human rubric on ten samples.
  3. Run Q4_K_M and your candidate formats; require the delta inside your tolerance per task class, not on average.
  4. Pin the chosen artifact by hash in gateway config, and re-run the protocol on every model or runtime upgrade.
